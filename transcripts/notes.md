## 1. Training Philosophy & Overall Approach

- **Central thesis: the marathon is "art, not science."** Science (VO2max, lactate threshold, heart-rate zones, critical velocity) gives useful background but is "anti-diluvian" compared to what top coaches actually do; much of the popular sports science is contradictory, constantly revised, and — in Stazza's view — sometimes flatly wrong (e.g., he argues the "burning" sensation in hard efforts is not caused by H+/lactate byproducts as long taught, citing a recent Cancer Research paper on the Krebs cycle). Good coaches operate years ahead of the science, which later catches up and puts formal names/measurements on what experienced coaches already do intuitively.
- **Core axiom, repeated constantly: "appropriate stress → recovery → adaptation."** Every session must apply the right stress, be followed by real recovery, and only then does the adaptation (fitness gain) occur — the workout itself doesn't make you fitter, the recovery afterward does.
- **The "overload principle"**: must progressively increase training stress (distance, pace, reduced recovery) in an individualized, progressive, structured way. Distinguish simple *metabolic* overload from trickier *musculoskeletal* overload — the latter is where most injuries come from when coaches get it wrong.
- **"The runner always has to have space to grow into"** — regardless of age (20 or 60), don't front-load every tool/stimulus at once. Just because a tool exists in the toolbox (hill blasts, temporal flow, more mileage) doesn't mean it should be used now. Coaches should deliberately hold athletes back rather than maximize every session — described as "the art of coaching" / risk management.
- **Training is not one-size-fits-all.** Two runners chasing the identical goal time should not necessarily get identical programs — training must fit individual life factors (work/family schedule, days available, shift work, injury history, twitch-fibre profile). Every run/session should have a stated reason specific to that athlete.
- **Talent matters for track/middle-distance (1500m–5000m), NOT for the marathon.** You can't just turn up and be a good track runner without genuine talent (largely VO2max/genetic ceiling), but marathon success is driven far more by training structure, consistency, and discipline than by innate talent. Rough guide: sub-3:00 and sub-2:30 marathon achievable by "any reasonably fit person" with correct training; ~2:15–2:18 still mostly training/dedication; talent starts to matter more below ~2:10.
  - "Ceiling of talent" concept: everyone has an approximate performance ceiling shaped by genetics *and* life circumstances; the coach's job is to get the athlete as close to *their own* ceiling as possible, not to someone else's.
  - Diagnostic for "true" talent: compare short-distance PBs (1500m/3K/5K) to marathon-equivalent pace. If short-distance times are unremarkable relative to marathon pace, that's a "marathon-built" (not naturally gifted) athlete. "Frank Horwill's Rule of Four": a 4:00 1500m runner (64 sec/lap) "should" run ~68 sec/lap for 3000m and ~72 sec/lap for 5000m; when times don't decay this way, it signals an endurance-built rather than natural-speed athlete.
  - Big year-one improvements (4:00→sub-3:00 marathon, etc.) are described as "run of the mill" within this system — used explicitly to argue that large jumps reflect correcting basic training errors, not doping or rare genetic gift.
- **The "art of coaching" = pattern recognition + individualization + communication**, not just knowing sessions/science:
  - A coach integrates many simultaneous subtle cues (breathing, facial tension, foot strike, arm carriage, recovery quality between reps, verbal tone, emotional state) plus training/life history to make judgment calls ("she could handle one more rep today but it would compromise tomorrow").
  - Communication is "where the art comes in" — reading between the lines of what an athlete says/doesn't say matters as much as the Strava numbers.
  - A coach must be a therapist as much as a technician — ~80% of coaching is man-management/communication, not session design.
  - An athlete's life environment (family/work support, stress, sleep) is as important as raw talent for reaching goals.
- **What to look for in a coach (practical checklist)**:
  - Are the coach's athletes actually progressing, and do they stay/renew long-term (retention = good signal)?
  - Does the coach specialize in/have proven experience with your event and level? Check current *and* former athletes.
  - Communication should be open/frequent, not gated behind tiered pricing ("pay X to speak once a month" is a red flag).
  - Programs should be genuinely individualized weekly (not generic cut-and-paste monthly templates), adjustable daily.
  - Should include weekly feedback review and at least a monthly progress call explaining the "why," done collaboratively (not dogmatically top-down).
  - Reasonable pricing: Stazza charges ~£38–50/month; states nobody should pay more than ~£50–60/month for individualized coaching regardless of the coach's level (he coaches Olympians at this price) — anything like $100+/month is "a ripoff."
  - Red flag: coaches who just hand their *own* coach's programs to every athlete unmodified.
  - Bare-minimum coach background: some physical-therapy/physio knowledge, strength & conditioning knowledge, running-specific sports-nutrition knowledge, basic exercise-science understanding, real coaching experience.
  - Good coaches typically don't need to advertise heavily — growth comes from referrals and results.
- **Coaching process used in this stable**: an in-depth "Profile Questionnaire" (~10 pages: medical history, injury/S&C history, nutrition, current/previous training, goals, SWOT) plus a 5-test "ancillary assessment" → 1-hour onboarding call → individualized weekly training (posted weekly, not monthly/generic) → weekly feedback form + coach voice-note review → monthly review call discussing the "why," done as a two-way dialogue, not dogmatically.
- **Kenyan/"running by feel" culture held up as the philosophical ideal**: many elite Kenyan groups train with minimal or no technology (no continuous HR/GPS, many run standard sessions without a watch); intensity/session length/block duration is adjusted on observed daily readiness rather than a rigid calendar; veteran runners in a training group provide a "human monitoring system" (watching form/breathing/expression) that can catch things devices can't; training is synced to natural rhythms (temperature, daylight) rather than the clock. Quoted Kenyan-coach line: *"the watch cannot fill your muscles or your breath — only you can know these things."* Not anti-technology in principle (elite Kenyan training increasingly uses sports science too) — the point is prioritizing perceptual foundations first.

## 2. Periodization / Phases of a Training Year

### High-level annual structure (the core "Stazza's Stable" system)
Run in this order, each phase building the prerequisite for the next:

1. **Foundation phase** (true beginners / long-plateaued runners only)
2. **Pre-Super Base**
3. **Super Base** (~14 weeks / ~4 months)
4. **Marathon Bridge** (or "half-marathon bridge" if the near-term goal is a half) — a few months, length flexible
5. **Marathon-specific training block** (~11 weeks)
6. **Taper** (final ~10 days) → **Race**
7. **Recovery** (3+ weeks) → repeat

Entry point by level: true beginners/long-term-plateaued start at Foundation→Pre-Super-Base; intermediate runners (roughly 3:00–2:30 marathon shape) generally skip Pre-Super-Base and enter directly at Super Base (Pre-Super-Base is revisited only after a long injury layoff); elite runners still benefit from a Super Base block too, not just recreational athletes.

### One marathon per year (central recommendation)
- Common mistake: 2–3 marathons/year, copying what elites do — but elites race more often for money/sponsorship, and even they rarely exceed 2 marathons/year at genuine peak effort (Kipchoge ~2/year is described as "the upper limit" even for elite recovery capacity; Paula Radcliffe often raced just one/year — her 2:15:25 WR at London 2003 came off a season with no prior marathon).
- Rationale (with cited physiological detail from Stazza's book): muscle-fiber structural damage from a hard marathon takes ~4–6 weeks to fully repair; inflammation markers follow a similar timeline; stress-hormone disruption and reduced glycogen-supercompensation capacity last ~6–8 weeks — all far longer than the days/couple of weeks it takes for subjective soreness to fade. Stacking marathons before true recovery produces a "training harder but performing worse" trap.
- Legitimate exceptions to one-a-year: experienced ultrarunners using a marathon as a sub-maximal training run; "pure experience seekers" prioritizing travel/participation over performance; late-career athletes shifting from performance-development to performance-expression. The issue is a mismatch between approach and expectations, not frequency itself.
- Typical annual cycle: (1) post-marathon recovery 4–6 weeks; (2) half-marathon development phase ~12–28 weeks (Super Base + Bridge, lower volume than marathon-specific but more threshold/tempo work, occasional 5K/10K racing); (3) transition (~2 weeks) into marathon-specific prep (~12–16 weeks).
- Year-over-year improvement benchmarks (rough planning heuristic): 4:00 marathoner → 20–40 min/year; 3:30 → sub-3:00 or close; 3:00 → ~2:45; 2:45 → 2:30; 2:30 → 2:25 (diminishing returns as level rises — roughly "one notch" per year with correct training). Good responders can sometimes skip a whole notch (e.g., someone freshly under 3:00 jumping straight to high-2:30s/low-2:40s the next year) if training stays healthy and consistent.
- 12-week-out benchmark: race a 10K or half marathon hard ~12 weeks before the goal marathon — it's both a tune-up and the main marathon-pace predictor (see §5).
- **Do NOT race a half marathon or 5K in the final 3–6 weeks before the marathon** "to sharpen up" — trains the body to burn glycogen at the wrong effort and is a red flag if it goes *well* (means you're in half-marathon shape, not marathon shape). Costs roughly 5 lost quality sessions once you count the taper (10 days) and recovery (~1 week) around it. If you feel you need a tune-up race in the block, run it *at marathon pace* with extra miles tacked on rather than racing it all-out. (Kipchoge doesn't race half-marathons/5Ks in the weeks before a marathon — "there's a reason for that.")

### Foundation phase (true beginners only)
- Goal: get to running comfortably **3× 65 min easy + 1× 90 min long run, easy**, 4×/week (progress duration 5–10 min/week depending on age/injury history/life schedule). No pace/tempo work in the long run yet — pure duration tolerance.
- Purpose: develop the musculoskeletal system for running specifically (even ex-rowers/cyclists/footballers with good aerobic capacity usually lack running-specific durability) — this is where most beginner injuries (shin splints, etc.) happen if skipped or rushed.
- Rationale for why beginners get injured when given intervals/long runs straight away: most runners slower than ~3:10–3:30 are "hip-flexor dominant" with a shuffling stride (hip flexors are relatively weak "puppet strings," so calves/shins absorb excess load) — need to build durability *and* fix the movement pattern before adding speed.

### Pre-Super Base (~7 weeks; "the bit that turns a shuffler into a runner")
- Structure once at 4 easy days/week: keep 1×65min easy + the 90min long run (still easy/no pace work); convert the other 2 days into a **hill-blast session** and an **aerobic-strides session** (see §4 for full technique detail).
- Purpose: rewire running form/economy **autonomously** (not via conscious cueing or gym strength work) — get the glutes engaged and hip extension increased, moving the runner from a hip-flexor-dominant shuffle toward "running like a kid in a playground" again.
- Outcome markers: heart rate at the same easy pace drops slightly (an economy signal, not a fitness signal); easy-run pace often gets faster without trying. This is a *technical* improvement, not a fitness one — Super Base adds the fitness layer next.
- Do NOT give beginners traditional club-style VO2max sessions (8×1K@5K pace, 5×1K@3K pace, 10×400@1500m pace) — designed for early-20s middle-distance specialists, causes injury in this population.

### Super Base (~14 weeks / ~4 months) — "the best-kept secret in running"
Full detail and worked numeric example in §4. High-level: one midweek tempo-based session + one long "endurance spine" run per week, everything else pure easy recovery. Builds musculoskeletal durability + aerobic capacity via generous-recovery, controlled-pace sessions (never "eyeballs out"). By the end, the athlete's original 20-min tempo pace should be matched or beaten over a full 60-min long-run tempo — and ~95% of finishers run 10K/10-mile (and often half-marathon) PBs as a side effect, even though the block isn't designed for racing.
- Do NOT race during the Super Base or Pre-Super-Base — it isn't designed for it; benchmark with a half marathon/10K only once it's finished.

### Marathon Bridge (or half-marathon bridge)
- Purpose: transition the raw strength built in the Super Base into race-specific speed across 5K–half-marathon paces, in preparation for marathon-specific training.
- Structurally: recoveries shorten (vs. Super Base's generous recoveries) and sessions become varied/mixed-pace rather than single steady reps — e.g., Canova-style cut-downs (10-8-6-4-2 min descending, roughly HM-effort down to 5K-effort, off 2min recovery), pyramids (1-2-3-4-5-4-3-2-1 min off 2min), or split tempos (15min tempo + 3min easy + 10×1min@5K effort/1min jog + 3min easy + 15min tempo again).
- Endurance-spine long runs continue but alternate fortnightly with "structural runs" (slower pace, longer duration — see §4) as sessions intensify, to keep injury risk down while building marathon-specific strength.
- Good time to race 5K/10K occasionally to break up training and touch faster paces (not a dedicated 5K block — see §4/hills).
- This is where "temporal flow" fatigue-resistance training is introduced *if the athlete is ready* (established base, no current injury issues) — see §4.
- Length flexible: e.g., ~3 months before a marathon block, or 6–8-week blocks each capped by a taper+race+recovery cycle to avoid one long unbroken grind.

### Marathon-specific block (~11 weeks) — full detail in §5 (race-day/marathon-specific section) since it's built entirely around marathon-pace long runs.

### Post-marathon / post-big-effort recovery (used after every marathon, and after any big HM/10K peak)
1. **1 full week completely off running** (7–10 days ideal; even 7 helps a lot).
2. **1–2 weeks easy-only rebuilding**: progressive easy-run durations (e.g., 35→45→55→65→80 min) back up to the normal baseline (3×65min/1×90min).
3. **~week 4**: first light quality test — e.g., 20min wu + 20min@10-mile effort + 20min cd, plus a moderate long run at HM-effort-minus-a-bit — used as the signal readiness to resume real training.
4. Only after this ~3-week window should hard sessions resume. Racing/training hard again too soon is a common mistake — you're in marathon shape, not 5K/10K shape, right after a marathon, and pushing risks injury/burnout even if a short-term PB sneaks through.
5. A missed session or forced rest (illness, life event) inside a block isn't catastrophic — treat it like an involuntary taper; fitness often "catapults forward" once resumed, the same as a planned cutback week.

## 3. Easy / Aerobic Running & Pacing Philosophy

- **Easy = conversational pace, full stop.** You should be able to hold a conversation the way you would sitting in a room; if you're breathing hard or straining, slow down. This rule applies from complete beginners up to Olympians (e.g., cited example: even elite marathoners' easy runs are capped around 7:50/mile).
- **Explicit easy-run pace ceiling used with every athlete, beginner to Olympian: no faster than 7:50/mile** on an easy day (many are advised toward 8:15/mile to be safe). Start runs slower (~8:30–8:45/mi) and let effort/pace drift down naturally rather than planning a fast finish.
- There is **no such thing as "too slow"** for an easy day — cited examples: a 2:12 marathoner easy-running at 9:00/mile; sub-3:00-capable runners happy at 9:00+/mile easy. The benefit is fewer injuries and consistently-hit hard sessions, which matters more than easy-run speed.
- **Do not use heart-rate zones, "zone 2," power, or lactate testing to define easy running** — Stazza considers most of this either unnecessary or built on outdated science; the entire philosophy is to run by *feel* and use data only as an occasional calibration check, not a moment-to-moment guide (see the "Wisdom of Feel" philosophy below).
- Easy-run duration: **65 minutes is the practical daily ceiling** for anyone below elite level. Rationale: 35–55 min of easy running gives roughly the same aerobic benefit (diminishing differences in that range); real *extra* physiological benefit only starts to appear around 60–65 min — but going past 65 min risks compromising recovery for the next key session, so 65 min is the sweet spot, not a hard physiological limit. Long runs are capped similarly at ~2h05m outside of marathon/ultra-specific blocks (beyond that point recovery cost rises faster than benefit and compromises the following week).
- **Frequency/mileage**: mileage is *not* the limiting factor for most goals. Concrete proof-points repeated throughout: sub-3:00 marathon achievable on ~50 miles/week, 5 days/week, no doubles (real example: a runner averaging ~50mi/week went 2:52→2:28 in a year); sub-2:30 achievable on ~80km/week, 4×/week for some athletes; one athlete qualified for the US Olympic Marathon Trials running only ~25 miles/week. **Runners slower than ~2:25 marathon shape should run singles only** — doubles are reserved for sub-2:25/elite-level athletes. Most sub-3:00 candidates run just 5×/week (some 6×); 7 days/week or double-days are generally unnecessary and often counter-productive below elite level (contrasted explicitly against "Norwegian doubles" methodology, which Stazza considers overkill/injury-prone for non-elites).
- **"Running by feel" is the philosophical foundation**, laid out at length in a dedicated book-excerpt episode:
  - Data (HR, pace, power) only captures fragments and has "measurement lag" — periodic snapshots vs. the body's continuous internal feedback, which integrates far more variables (proprioception, effort quality, environment, emotional state) than any device.
  - Build a personal "feel" vocabulary over time: distinguish cardiovascular strain vs. muscular fatigue, sustainable discomfort vs. unsustainable distress, and specific fatigue signatures (e.g., "quad showing early-stage fatigue in landing pattern").
  - Calibrate feel against data only periodically (not continuously) — e.g., a weekly "technology-free run" (leave the watch home, guess your effort/split, then check after), occasional HR spot-checks, standard-route time trials, or a "perception journal" logging increasingly specific sensations.
  - "Progressive technological independence" model: (1) full metrics every session while learning → (2) systematically compare perceived vs. measured effort → (3) technology-free standard sessions with periodic checks → (4) metrics only for specific purposes → (5) mostly internal feedback with occasional calibration checks. Devices remain useful for: novel terrain/conditions, early-stage runners still building vocabulary, returning from injury (re-establishing baselines), and specific biomechanical development work.
  - Marathon-specific case for feel over data: (1) the "pacing paradox" — optimal marathon pace is a narrow band (slightly fast = catastrophic fade, slightly slow = wasted potential), and early-race heart rate is often artificially elevated by adrenaline, making it a poor pacing guide; (2) fueling ("gut wisdom") — optimal carb intake varies by individual and by day, so real-time digestive feedback beats a rigid "X grams/hour" formula; (3) mid-race adaptability to unexpected events (weather, pack dynamics, hydration, discomfort) requires internal reference points a purely device-trained runner lacks.
  - A coach's complementary "clinical eye": subtle external cues (lowered arm carriage, reduced knee lift, altered foot strike, facial/jaw tension, and especially *how smoothly* an athlete transitions between effort levels) can reveal fatigue/limits before the athlete admits or notices it — and part of a coach's job is explicitly teaching the athlete to recognize these same patterns in themselves over time (a 5-step transfer: coach observes → describes it → athlete links it to a sensation → athlete detects it independently → athlete eventually predicts/prevents it).

## 4. Key Workouts and Their Purpose

### 4a. Hill Blasts — "the best bang-for-buck session in running," core to nearly every phase
- **Structure**: 8 seconds near-maximal effort uphill + **2:30 walk-back recovery** (not jogged) — this exactly matches the creatine-phosphate energy system: ~8–12 sec of stored CP per max effort, ~2:30 to fully replenish (analogy: sprinters doing 50m flat-out reps with long walking recoveries between, not laziness).
- **Hill grade**: shallow, ~5% (a highway-overpass-type incline), NOT steep — steep hills for masters/older runners increase injury risk and encourage leaning/hip-flexor dominance instead of glute drive.
- **Age rule**: under ~30 (some say ~35) can use a steeper hill and just sprint flat-out; over that age, use the shallow hill + the technique cues below.
- **Technique mantra: "Tall, Relaxed, Piston, Pop-Pop-Pop"**
  - *Tall* — run upright/perpendicular to the slope rather than leaning into the hill; leaning recruits hip flexors more, running tall recruits glutes more (you can feel this even sitting in a chair by sitting upright vs. slouching).
  - *Relaxed* — near-max effort (~95–98%, not literally 100%) but stay breathing and not tense.
  - *Piston* — lightly clenched fists, arms drive straight forward/back (not across the body) up to shoulder height; critically, drive the elbow back and brush the top of the hip on the backswing — this rear-arm action is the single most important cue, lengthening stride and engaging glutes (again, demonstrable seated).
  - *Pop-pop-pop* — minimize ground-contact time (uphill running naturally increases contact time; this cue counteracts it) — think "hot coals."
  - Correct execution feels like pedaling a bicycle uphill, heels coming up under the glutes, slightly awkward/unnatural at first.
- **Preceded by 2–3 drills** (high knees, straight-leg march — 20m + walk back, then a 20m stride + walk back, repeated) to "drill" the movement pattern into the stride immediately before the hill reps.
- **Progression**: start at 4 reps → 6 → hold at 8 reps (more is not better — by rep 7–8 the CNS is fatigued and further reps give diminishing returns/added injury risk).
- **Do NOT do hill blasts in race week** — use strides instead (less ground-contact stress).
- **Video yourself** to check form (perpendicular posture, hip extension, heel under glutes) — "practice doesn't make perfect, perfect practice makes perfect."
- Purpose: improves running economy/efficiency, stride length and power, recruits fast-twitch fibers — considered one of the highest-value sessions for marathoners specifically, because marathon performance is fundamentally about economy. Works synergistically with aerobic strides (below) — pace gains from hill blasts show up as faster strides without extra effort.
- A full 25–30 min warm-up is needed beforehand given the intensity.

### 4b. Aerobic Strides (credit: coach John Kellogg) — pairs with hill blasts in Pre-Super-Base
- A misnomer (all energy systems are used to varying degrees) but named for the generous recovery.
- **Progression** (weekly): Week 1: 3 sets of 5×20sec @ ~5K effort from a landmark (e.g., lamppost) to another landmark, jog back each rep, 3–4min jog between sets. Week 2: 5×30sec. Week 3: 5×40sec (last 2 reps of set 3 start to feel like work). Week 4: repeat 3×5×40sec, then add a continuous "add-on" effort at 5K effort after a further 3–4min jog: 2min. Week 5: add-on → 3min. Week 6: → 4min. Week 7: → 5min (by now the add-on alone is often faster than the entire original week-1 session — attributed to hill-blast-driven economy gains transferring in). The 4–5 min add-ons start to resemble a genuine traditional workout.
- Purpose: reintroduces "free," childlike running (vs. shuffling) safely, in small controlled doses, without the injury risk of jumping straight to standard interval sessions (6×800, 6×1mile, etc.) that most recreational marathoners aren't ready for.

### 4c. Super Base — the two weekly sessions, worked example (cleanest version, from appended interview)
Example at a 5:00/km ("could hold this pace for 1 hour today") starting tempo pace:

**Midweek tempo/interval session progression:**
- Wk1: 20min wu + 20min tempo @5:00/km + 20min cd.
- Wk2: 20min tempo @5:00/km + 3min easy + 10min @5:00/km (second block added).
- Wk3–4: extend the second block to 15min, then 20min (now 2×20min @5:00/km) — athlete should feel a strength shift (not speed, robustness).
- Wk5: 20min tempo @5:00/km + 3min easy + 4×5min @4:54/km (6 sec/km faster) w/3min recovery.
- Wk6: drop the leading 20-min tempo → 8×5min @4:54/km w/3min recovery = **"the precipice of the V-shaped valley of death"** (about to descend into faster/shorter reps).
- Wk7: 10×4min @4:51/km (+3 sec/km only, since reps are shorter) w/3min recovery.
- Wk8: 10×3min @4:48/km w/3min recovery.
- Wk9: 15×2min @4:45/km w/2min recovery.
- Wk10 (bottom of the "V"): 20×1min @4:42/km w/1min recovery (~5K-equivalent pace, but safe because time spent at that intensity per rep is short).
- Climb back UP the same ladder (15×2, 10×3, 10×4, 8×5) — crucially, pace does **not** slow back down symmetrically: each rung on the way up matches or beats the pace hit on the way down, because the endurance-spine long runs underneath have been "holding it together." (Avoid classic single-set 5K sessions like 8×1K or 4×1mile off big recoveries — designed for young milers/5K specialists, not marathon-shape runners.)
- Generous recoveries throughout are deliberate — the point is maintaining good form at a controlled pace, not racing the session; the weekend long run does the "gluing together" of fitness.
- Self-check after every rep: you should feel you could still do more — never "eyeballs out."

**Weekend "endurance spine" long-run progression (same worked example):**
- Pace = 12 sec/km (20 sec/mile) SLOWER than the midweek tempo pace.
- Wk1: 30min easy + 40min @5:12/km + 30min easy.
- Wk2: 50min @5:12/km. Wk3: 60min @5:12/km.
- Wk4: back to 40min but 3 sec/km faster (5:09/km) — cycle repeats: 40/50/60@5:09 → 40/50/60@5:06 → 40/50/60@5:03, etc. (a rolling ~3-week cycle).
- Self-check: after finishing any tempo segment, you should honestly feel you could hold that same pace another ~80–90 minutes on that day/course/conditions — if not, you went out too hard (most people naturally undershoot this conservatism, so err conservative).
- Periodically insert a **"structural run"** instead of the endurance-spine run: longer duration (80–90 min) at a pace dropped back further (~20 sec/mile slower than half-marathon effort, i.e., slower than endurance-spine pace) — builds musculoskeletal durability specifically ahead of marathon-specific work, since you don't want your longest pre-marathon-block run to be only 13–14 miles.
- **Endurance Spine vs. Structural Run, defined**: Endurance Spine = ~10 sec/mile slower than current half-marathon effort, 40/50/60min. Structural Run = ~20 sec/mile slower than HM effort (slower again), but longer duration (80–90min). Both trace back to adapting Canova's famous "25K @ 98% effort every fortnight" (given to a 5000m runner) into something safe for recreational/masters athletes.
- **Outcome**: by the end of the ~14-week Super Base, typical improvement on the endurance-spine tempo pace is ~30 sec/km, and the final 60-min tempo pace usually matches or beats the *original* 20-min tempo pace from week 1. ~95% of finishers run 10K/10-mile PBs (often half-marathon PBs too) even though the block isn't designed as race prep.
- Half-marathon outcome benchmarks by starting level (14-week Super Base alone, before any Bridge/marathon work): ~2:00 HM → sub-1:40; ~1:35–1:39 → sub-1:30; ~1:30 → ~1:25; ~1:25 → ~1:20; ~1:20 → ~1:17 (diminishing improvement chunks as level rises).
- **"Yo-yo" diagnostic tool**: if an athlete plateaus at a given rung of the ladder, dropping in an atypical stimulus (hill blasts if not yet introduced, or a different-structured session) reveals whether they lean slow-twitch — an immediate fitness jump confirms it and unlocks continued progress. Also usable generally to break any plateau.

### 4d. "Temporal Flow Training" — fatigue-resistance work (advanced/elite; NOT for beginners or early intermediates)
- Origin: named via conversation about "temporal" (time — past/present/future, i.e., Super Base/Bridge/marathon-specific) + "flow" (progression). Inspired by Renato Canova's big-volume float-recovery sessions (8×2K, 6×3K, 5×4K, 4×5K) — widely copied incorrectly by Western coaches who don't realize those sessions are designed for specific athletes at altitude, not sea-level recreational runners.
- Core idea: NOT "extending speed" (a common misreading of Canova) but building **fatigue resistance** — keeping running economy/form intact even when fatigued, via big combined-volume sessions run at a controlled (not max) pace.
- **Full progression ladder** (used at both sub-elite and true-elite levels, scaled by pace): 
  20×400m/200m jog (~5K effort) → 10×600m+10×400m/200m jog within, 400m jog between sets → 10×600+10×400+10×200 → 10×800+10×400+10×200 → 10×1K+10×400+10×200 (~10mi total, "quality but not eyeballs out") → 1×3K+3×2K+10×400 → 2×3K+3×2K+10×400 (all off 200m jog within reps, 400m jog between sets). Reps within a combined session are run slightly slower than if done as a standalone session, since fatigue compounds through the workout.
- Alternated fortnightly with non-temporal-flow weeks (10-mile/10K-effort sessions), per the "what you do today shows up in 10 days" overload-lag rule.
- Paired with **structural runs** (not more endurance-spine work) on the long-run side, since temporal-flow midweek sessions are so taxing the weekend run needs to be *less intense* (if similar/longer duration) to allow recovery.
- Threshold for use: solid injury-free multi-year base, ideally already running 80+ miles/week for the most advanced version; a "mild version" can be given to strong sub-elites (example: a 2:54 marathoner given a modified version ran 2:36 in the next block). Explicitly withheld from newer/faster-improving athletes even when they ask for it, if the current simpler approach is still working.

### 4e. Marathon Bridge session menu (concrete bank, post-Super-Base)
- **5K stimulus, safely**: 20×1min @5K effort/1min jog (≈20min total, broken into safe 1-min doses vs. traditional 4–5min reps that injure this population).
- **5-mile stimulus**: 5×5min @5-mile effort/2min jog.
- **10K stimulus**: take the Super-Base session (10×3min@10K effort/3min recovery) and cut recovery to 2:30, then 2:00.
- **10-mile stimulus**: 12×3min@10-mile effort/35sec recovery → (2 weeks later) 15×3min/35sec → 8×5min/75sec (faster runners) or 8×5min/2min (newer runners).
- **Varied filler/tempo session**: 15min@10-mile effort, 3min jog, 10×1min@5K effort/1min jog, 3min recovery, 15min tempo again.
- **Canova-style cut-down**: 10-8-6-4-2 min off 2min recovery, roughly HM-effort (10min) down to 5K-effort (2min).
- **"Lactate Grenades"** (aggressive, used sparingly, e.g., pre-breakthrough): repeated sets (8–10×) of a few minutes at marathon-pace-adjacent effort + 1min jog + 1min at a notably faster effort + a longer jog recovery (e.g., 3min) between sets — deliberately designed so the athlete "blows up" a little toward the end of each set; that controlled blow-up under fatigue *is* the stimulus. Progressed over a block (e.g., 10 sets of 4/1/1/3min → 8 sets of 5/1/1/3min).
- **"Lactate Shunts"** (progressive-overload endurance stimulus, used in the final weeks of a marathon or half-marathon block): continuous run alternating 1km @ marathon pace / 1km @ half-marathon pace back-to-back with **no recovery** between — e.g., 12km three weeks out building to 16–20km ~10 days out. Rationale given: the faster km spikes lactate, which the body then "shuttles" and uses as fuel during the following slower km — trains the lactate-shuttle mechanism directly. Not meant to make marathon pace itself faster (that's fixed at the block's start) — purpose is extending durability at that fixed pace.

### 4f. Hill session taxonomy (fuller picture beyond hill blasts)
- **Classic middle-distance hill reps** (young/track-focused runners only, NOT for most marathoners/masters): 5–6% grade. Progression 10×30sec → 10×45sec → 10×1min → 10×90sec → 8×2min → 6×3min (hills get longer through winter base). Too injury-risky (hamstring/Achilles) for older/slower runners who can't maintain form on longer hill reps at pace.
- **Hill+tempo blends** (younger/advanced runners): e.g., 10min tempo@10-mile effort + easy 2–3min + 10×30sec hill reps w/jog-back + easy 2–3min + another 10min tempo. Cap ~20min total tempo volume when combined with 2 sets of hill reps.
- **Passive hill strengthening**: gentle, easy-effort hills folded into ordinary easy runs (not "worked") — low-risk, long-term tendon/strength benefit, recommended for nearly everyone.
- **Race-specific hill prep** (Boston, New York, etc.): incorporate rolling terrain into long runs so the body adapts to switching uphill/downhill efficiently. Bring hilly long runs in EARLY in the block while still running slower than marathon pace (~20 sec/mile slower), building structural tolerance for 3–4 weeks *before* adding marathon-pace effort to hilly runs — diving straight into hilly+marathon-pace work risks injury. Uphill running burns more glycogen, so practice fueling on these runs too.
- **Advanced/elite descending hill ladder** (pre-marathon-block): 2×3min hills → 3×2min → 4×1min → 5×30sec (progressively shorter/faster); cap total volume ~10 minutes.
- 12×12-second hill reps off jog-back recovery is a distinct session type from the 8-second/2:30-recovery hill blast (different energy-system target/purpose), and 30-second hill reps are also used inside "broken tempo" sessions at certain points in the year.
- **"Broken tempo" session** (used for athletes needing more pace variety, e.g., a "one-gear" hard-running athlete): 15min@10-mile effort + 3min easy + 10×1min (flat or uphill) off jog-back + 3min easy + 15min@10-mile effort again — a moderate way to introduce gear-shifting without a full interval session.

### 4g. Long runs — purpose and structure (marathon-specific detail is in §5)
Stated in three explicit parts: (1) teaches the body to burn glycogen efficiently **at marathon pace specifically** (not general aerobic fitness); (2) builds musculoskeletal/structural capacity to handle the distance; (3) builds psychological confidence. A 20-mile *easy* run "has nothing to do with" running 26.2 miles at marathon pace — specificity is the whole point, hence the endurance-spine/structural-run/marathon-pace-run system rather than just "more slow miles."

### 4h. General session-design principles
- Never do a hard session the day before a long run to simulate "marathon fatigue" (a common UK/Ireland mistake) — insufficiently specific *and* a major unnecessary injury multiplier.
- Avoid the traditional club structure (interval Tue + tempo Thu + race Sat + long slow run Sun, often plus uncoordinated gym lifting) — "too much mess" in the week. The Stazza structure is deliberately minimal: one midweek workout + one long run with work built in, everything else pure recovery (aside from hill blasts/strides in the pre-base phase).
- VO2max-type sessions (5×1K@3K pace/2min recovery, 6×800, etc.) are explicitly NOT prescribed for marathon training — reserved for track/middle-distance events, due to poor injury-risk-to-benefit ratio for marathoners. VO2max itself is treated as a legitimate but *marathon-irrelevant* marker (Prof. Andy Jones was reportedly surprised Kipchoge's VO2max was "only" ~70 — reinforcing that fatigue resistance, not VO2max, is what actually matters for the marathon).
- Common review findings when inheriting an athlete from another coach/program: (1) lack of specificity (generic VO2max sessions, slow runs with no marathon-pace relevance); (2) marathon blocks made too long (fatigue/injury); (3) too aggressive a build-up. A training history dominated by "zone 2" and "threshold" work is cited as the most common cause of a prior plateau.

## 5. Race-Day / Marathon-Specific Pacing & Fueling Strategy

### Marathon-pace prediction ("what pace should I run?")
Several formulas appear across episodes, roughly consistent, tightening as the "old rule" is updated for modern shoes/fueling:
- **Old-school rule**: marathon pace = double half-marathon time + 10 min (men) / +8 min (women).
- **Updated rule (most repeated)**: marathon pace = double half-marathon time **+ 5–6 minutes**, for runners faster than ~3:00 half-marathon shape, *assuming* shoes/fueling/training are all dialed in. If your predicted/actual marathon pace comes out closer to the old +10 formula, that's read as a sign your training, fueling, or shoes need work.
- **First-timer/inexperienced-marathoner variant**: use the more conservative **+8–10 min** version rather than +5–6, since experience/pacing discipline hasn't been proven yet.
- **Simplified per-mile rule** (used constantly for setting long-run paces): marathon pace ≈ **20–25 sec/mile (≈12 sec/km) SLOWER than half-marathon race pace** for the sub-2:30-and-slower population (closer to half-marathon pace for elites). This is the version actually used to calculate long-run target paces throughout a block.
- The benchmark half-marathon race should ideally be run **~12–13 weeks out** from the goal marathon, all-out, in good conditions — it fixes marathon pace for the rest of the block (marathon pace is NOT re-calculated/chased faster mid-block; it's picked once from this race and then extended in distance).
- **Course-measurement nuance**: certified courses are measured along the shortest legal ("blue line") route, but real running (crowding, tangents, camber) adds distance — GPS watches typically show ~42.4–42.6+ km rather than 42.2. Train your marathon-pace target **~2–4 sec/mile (2 sec/km) faster on the watch** than the literal required pace, or you'll come up short/panic mid-race chasing splits.

### Marathon-specific long-run block (the core of the final ~11 weeks)
Worked template (paces relative to a fixed marathon pace "MP," picked from the 12-week-out half marathon):
- ~8 weeks out: 2mi wu + 12mi @20 sec/mi slower than MP + 2mi cd (should feel easy, since you just ran 13mi at ~20 sec/mi *faster* than MP in the benchmark half).
- Following weeks, alternating structural (slower/longer) and marathon-pace runs, narrowing the gap and extending distance: 14mi@15sec slower → 16mi@10sec slower → mixed runs blending MP miles with slower miles (e.g., 10mi@10sec slower + 6mi@MP, then flipped: 6mi@10sec slower + 10mi@MP) → 16mi@MP → **the two peak long runs**, typically ~4–5 weeks out: 3mi wu + 18mi @10sec slower than MP + 3mi cd (structural, ~24mi total) and 2mi wu + 18mi @ full MP + 2mi cd (~22mi total — "the key session," described as ~95% predictive of hitting the marathon goal barring bad weather/injury).
- **No midweek workout the week immediately following either 18-mile session** — full recovery week (easy running only), though the long run itself continues, now tapering: 16mi@MP → 14mi@MP → race-week 8mi@MP (all with 2mi wu/cd). These become weekly (not fortnightly) as race day approaches, purely dialing in pace/glycogen economy.
- Midweek sessions during this block stay around 10-mile/half-marathon effort (not faster) — e.g., 30min@HM effort progressing 30→35→40min across weeks, alternated with 10-mile-effort ladders (12×3min/35sec → 15×3min/35sec → 8×5min/1min) — supporting, not competing with, the marathon-pace long run. This "funnel" (faster-than-MP midweek work + slower-than-MP long runs) converges on MP itself by about 6 weeks out.
- At altitude, apply a consistent offset: marathon EFFORT there corresponds to running ~10 sec/mile SLOWER than the true sea-level-equivalent pace, applied to both midweek reps and long runs throughout.
- Elite/sub-elite midweek progression (heavier volume, still marathon-adjacent): 8×2K → 6×3K → 5×4K → 4×5K, at paces converging toward MP as reps lengthen (2K reps ~10sec/mile faster than MP; 3K reps ~50sec slower; 4K ~40sec slower; 5K ~30sec slower) — Canova-derived, only appropriate at altitude and for advanced/elite athletes, NOT recreational sea-level marathoners.

### Race-day execution
- **"Rule of Three" pacing strategy** (named, generalizable — originally for half marathon, applies to marathon too): split the race into three sections — (1) first ~3 miles/5K: run 5–10 sec/mile SLOWER than goal average pace, don't go out at goal pace; (2) middle section: settle into actual goal average pace; (3) final section: push on/race it in. Most blow-ups happen because runners misjudge the effort gap and go out too close to a shorter-race pace; banking comfort (not time) early lets you accelerate late instead of fading. Fading late is when panicked gel use backfires (blood diverted from skin-cooling/muscles to digestion, slowing you further).
- **Course-specific management (Boston example, generalizes to any net-downhill-start course like London)**: the first 3–4 miles are net downhill — deliberately run them slower than goal pace (a "freebie"/warm-up), don't bank time. Don't be fooled into thinking the whole middle section is a steady descent — it's often rolling ("undulating"), so run it by *feel*, not by chasing exact splits. Course/energy management before the late hills (e.g., Heartbreak Hill ~mile 20) determines whether the hill itself is a problem, not the hill in isolation. A downhill course like Boston is often not certified for records/qualifying purposes.
- **Mid-race pacing discipline**: don't decide "today's my day, let's push" right at halfway — a very common mistake (illustrated by a real elite case: surging with 2 miles to go toward a big PB, then the whole body seizing up in the final mile). Better to hold pace through 18–20 miles, assess, and if picking up the pace, do so gradually in small increments (a couple of sec/mile at a time), remaining cautious even at 23–24 miles since a lot can still go wrong.
- **"Silent killer": dew point, not just temperature/humidity.** If dew point ≈ air temperature (roughly anything above ~20°C where the two match), the body can't evaporate sweat efficiently — sweat sits on the skin "like cling film," trapping heat, forcing the heart/lungs to work harder; the wheels typically come off around 6–10K into the race once core temperature climbs. A real Olympic-marathon example: organizers moved a start an hour earlier specifically to dodge heat, which *raised* the dew point and made things worse — reinforcing that dew point (not raw temperature) should drive pacing decisions, and that intuitive "avoid the heat" fixes can backfire. In genuinely bad dew-point conditions, pace may need to be dialed back by 30–45+ sec/mile from sea-level-equivalent shape — far more conservative than most runners intuitively choose. Runners who deliberately go out slower than planned MP in bad conditions tend to finish fine (often still a PB); those who ignore dew point and go out at/near MP suffer badly late.
- **Course selection matters as much as training**: pick races/conditions matched to your goal — a hilly, hot race is a bad choice for a hard time-attack even with perfect training (real case: an athlete blew up on a hilly, hot course chasing sub-3 despite being warned off it, finishing 3:15 instead of the low-2:50s he was fit for).

## 6. Nutrition & Hydration

- **Fueling advances are as significant as "super shoes"** for modern marathon-time improvements — modern gels/drinks (Maurten, Precision Hydration, Mountain Fuel, UCAN, SIS, High5) allow ~80–90g carbs/hour at marathon pace vs. the old ~50–60g that caused GI distress. Attributing all modern time drops purely to shoe technology is called out as wrong/lazy commentary.
- **When to start practicing race fueling in training**: once a given training run will exceed **~2 hours** — below that it's optional/practice only; above 2 hours (serious marathon-pace long runs) it's necessary to rehearse exactly what you'll do on race day.
- **Gel frequency**: modern guidance is roughly every ~5K/~20min (more frequent than the old "every 30 min" rule). Alternating gel brands/textures (e.g., Maurten with a cheaper gel like High5) helps when one brand's texture becomes unpalatable after several gels in a row. Carrying a soft flask with an electrolyte+carb mix is sensible insurance against inconsistent aid-station cups.
- **Electrolytes**: for races/efforts under ~4 hours, dedicated electrolyte supplementation is not physiologically necessary — the body has enough stored; it's really about carbohydrate intake. Electrolytes matter more genuinely once you're past ~4 hours (ultra territory). Below that, perceived electrolyte benefit is largely placebo.
- **"Marathon is about glycogen and marathon pace, those two things"** — the entire long-run system exists to teach the body to burn glycogen *efficiently at marathon pace specifically*. Analogy: marathon pace training = driving a Volvo economically on a long motorway trip; racing 5Ks/doing short fast bursts mid-block = "a boy racer doing burnouts" — teaches the wrong metabolic pattern and should be avoided during a marathon block.
- **Recovery nutrition**: after long runs AND midweek workouts (not needed after plain easy runs), take a recovery drink within ~15 minutes, ideally a **3:1 carbohydrate-to-protein ratio** — a straight protein shake is a common mistake. Becomes more important with age (a personal anecdote: skipping this after a hard session post-layoff left the coach unable to walk for a week; resuming it fixed the problem immediately).
- **Iron supplementation — important caution**: do not take iron supplements just because generic "runners need iron" advice suggests it — only supplement if a blood test confirms low ferritin/iron. Men store iron efficiently and rarely need it; low iron is flagged more often in women (menstrual-cycle factors). Retest after ~3–4 months of therapy and stop once levels normalize. Don't take iron together with calcium (e.g., with milk/porridge) — they compete for absorption and calcium wins, wasting the dose; pair iron with vitamin C instead to aid absorption. Unexplained fatigue or underperforming against known training paces is a legitimate reason to request blood tests, not something to feel is "not a real reason" to bother a doctor.
- **Creatine**: limited direct evidence it helps marathon-specific performance (stronger evidence for explosive/power/sprint events); if used, skip the loading phase (maintenance dose cited around 3g/day women, 5g/day men — verify units/dosing independently before use). One athlete anecdotally felt improved recovery, though confounded with simultaneous life-stress reduction, ADHD treatment, and better sleep.
- **Weight management for overweight beginners**: target a moderate ~500–700 kcal/day deficit → ~1–2 lb/week loss (no crash dieting); prioritize food quality (vegetables, fruit, lean protein, whole grains; cut added sugar/processed food/refined carbs) over precise calorie counting; monitor rate of loss so it doesn't outpace what training load can support — find a sustainable "optimum weight," not a minimum.
- **Super shoes**: reserve for racing and key workouts, not daily easy training (people running slow daily miles in racing super shoes is called "more money than sense" — use a well-fitted daily trainer for easy days). Practice key workouts *in* the race-day shoes beforehand — cramping late in a race is theorized to be partly caused by muscles not yet adapted to how a carbon plate changes firing patterns/loading, on top of simply not being conditioned to hold that exact pace for that exact duration yet.

## 7. Recovery, Sleep, and Injury Prevention

### Recovery & sleep principles
- **Sleep is "your best workout"/the "secret weapon"** — actual fitness adaptation happens during rest and sleep, not during the session itself. A midday nap (~30 min) is valuable for recovery and injury-risk reduction. Runners who boast about performing well on minimal sleep would perform even better with adequate sleep; chronic under-sleeping correlates with nagging injuries, plateaus, and messier personal lives generally.
- **Rest day rule**: take an unplanned rest day at the first sign of excess fatigue/ache — cheap insurance against a forced, longer layoff later. There's no harm in an extra day off.
- **Illness protocol (colds, COVID, etc.)**: if sick, rest immediately — don't run through it; running while sick doesn't help fitness and prolongs the illness. Long-COVID risk was specifically linked to (a) training through COVID even when mild/asymptomatic, or (b) resting appropriately but then returning too quickly/aggressively afterward. Return protocol used successfully with ~350+ athletes: rest until symptoms clear AND testing negative AND feeling 100%; day 1 back — 30–40 min walk including a couple of gentle inclines, specifically monitoring breathing and heart rate on the inclines as the go/no-go signal; if fine, next day a 30-min easy run, then progress to 40/50/60 min over the first week back; week 2 back — hold normal daily duration, add a modest 20-min tempo (started slower than marathon effort, working down toward half-marathon effort) mid-week; if that's fine, add a slightly longer tempo on the weekend long run. By ~week 3 back, most people (~95–96%) are fully recovered — attributed to this group training mostly in aerobic (10-mile/half-marathon effort) rather than anaerobic zones, which take longer to recover from. Red flag for lingering issues: resting heart rate ~10–12 bpm higher than normal for an easy-run effort → stop immediately and rest longer.
- **Post-marathon (or any big race) recovery**: minimum 3 full weeks before resuming hard training (roughly 1 week fully off + 2 weeks easy) — even though subjective soreness resolves faster, deeper recovery (muscle-fiber repair ~4–6 weeks, hormonal/glycogen-system disruption ~6–8 weeks per cited research) takes much longer. Racing again too soon after an all-out marathon is a mistake — marathon fitness ≠ 5K/10K fitness.
- **Mid-block recovery after a big peak effort** (e.g., a hard half-marathon mid-training-block): take a week off + a week easing back — doesn't cost fitness, and often "catapults" fitness forward afterward (like a pulled-back catapult being released).

### Injury-prevention general principles
- Respect a ~10% rule for weekly mileage/intensity increases.
- Sudden changes are the #1 trigger across nearly all injuries: mileage/intensity spikes, surface changes (grass→road, cambered roads), worn-out shoes, muscle imbalances (especially weak glutes), inadequate warm-up, dehydration/fatigue.
- Replace running shoes roughly every 300–500 miles.
- Cross-training (swim/bike) can substitute for a run when fatigue is elevated, maintaining fitness while lowering injury risk.
- Escalate to a physical therapist/physio/osteopath early if something seems to be turning chronic, rather than self-treating indefinitely.
- ~80% of runners experience an injury at some point — it's normal, and most are preventable/treatable if caught early.

### 10 most common running injuries (countdown, with cause/prevention/acute care)
1. **Achilles tendonitis** — pain/stiffness at back of ankle, worse mornings/run-onset. Causes: sudden mileage/intensity jump, tight calves, bad shoes. Avoid speed work/hills while symptomatic but keep it loaded (full rehab is separate). Acute: ice massage directly on the point of pain, anti-inflammatories for 48h if not contraindicated (e.g. asthma), gentle calf stretching post-run.
2. **Stress fractures** — tiny bone cracks (shins/feet/hips); gradual deep ache, worse with activity, better with rest; often invisible on early X-rays. Higher risk: too-much-too-soon on hard surfaces, women with irregular periods/low bone density/poor nutrition, worn shoes. Prevention: gradual buildup, rest days, varied surfaces, adequate calcium/vitamin D. Never run through a suspected stress fracture.
3. **Hamstring strains** — mild pull to full tear; sharp pain, sometimes an audible "pop." Occurs during speed work/hills or late in fatigued long runs/tempos as form breaks down. Prevention: strengthen hamstrings AND glutes, flexibility work, dynamic warm-ups before hard sessions. Acute: ice, rest, then a genuinely gradual graded return (don't rush back).
4. **Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome)** — dull inner-shin ache; the classic "too much too soon" injury, especially after time off or a surface change. Flat feet/overpronation at higher risk (a structured/supportive shoe can help). Prevention: gradual buildup, shoe replacement, softer surfaces, calf/shin strengthening. Treatment: STOP running (cross-train instead), don't fear several full days off. (Note: overly tight shoelaces can mimic similar top-of-foot pain via a different mechanism.)
5. **Calf strains** — occur at the muscle-tendon junction; feels like being kicked in the calf; bruising (if present) may appear *below* the actual injury site (gravitational tracking). Common in "weekend warriors" loading hard on consecutive days. Can be confused with referred nerve pain from the back (via piriformis) — foam rolling won't fix that; needs professional diagnosis. Acute: stop, ice, elevate, avoid forced stretching in the first 48h.
6. **Hip flexor strains** — front-of-hip/groin pain, worse lifting the knee or rising from sitting. Can also be a simple muscle spasm rather than a true strain. Caused by weak glutes + tight hip flexors (common with desk jobs), triggered by hill/speed work. Prevention: couch-stretch-style hip-flexor stretching, glute strengthening, movement breaks if desk-bound.
7. **Plantar fasciitis** — sharp stabbing heel pain, worst with first morning steps. Often triggered by ANY footwear change, not just running shoes (e.g., dress shoes/heels). Prevention: calf/plantar-fascia stretching (AIS method), proper supportive footwear. Caution: aggressive tennis-ball/frozen-bottle rolling can aggravate the underlying inflammation — use gently; avoid barefoot walking on hard surfaces. Notoriously slow/stubborn to resolve — patience required; escalate to a professional if it persists beyond a week.
8. **IT band syndrome** — sharp/burning outer-knee pain, often not until several miles in, worse downhill. The band is *attached* to the bone (not just rubbing against it) — pain often comes from the band fraying or a tight TFL (hip) pulling tension through it; weak hips let the leg rotate inward, increasing tension. Prevention: strengthen hips/glutes (clamshells, side-lying leg raises, single-leg bridges), avoid cambered roads/downhill running while symptomatic. **Critical: never foam-roll the IT band itself** — it's an avascular, taut ligament-like structure; rolling doesn't help and can worsen irritation (sharp pain there is often a fat pad, not something to "work through"). Roll the surrounding muscles instead; fix the root cause (usually hip weakness) via a professional.
9. **Patellofemoral pain syndrome ("runner's knee")** — pain around/behind the kneecap, worse on stairs/sitting/downhill, sometimes a grinding sensation. Root cause: poor kneecap tracking from a weak VMO (inner quad muscle) plus weak hips/glutes and/or tight lateral leg structures. Prevention: quad strengthening (isometric holds ~8 sec, straight-leg raises, wall sits) with VMO focus, hip-flexor/quad flexibility, avoid sudden mileage/downhill spikes and cambered roads. Generally responds well to conservative treatment.
10. **General muscle strains/pulls (the single most common category overall)** — graded 1 (mild) to 3 (complete rupture); a high hamstring tendinopathy near the pelvis that's trained through can progress to a full rupture — a specific danger to heed. Causes: muscle imbalances, poor flexibility, inadequate warm-up, dehydration, fatigue, previous inadequately-rehabbed injuries recurring. Acute: classic RICE (rest/ice/compression/elevation); graded return to avoid converting a minor strain into a major tear.

## 8. Strength Training / Supplementary Work

- **Overall stance: minimal, and often unnecessary for most runners.** Weightlifting is described as "one of the biggest con jobs going" in running for two reasons: (1) most people — including many personal trainers — don't lift with good technique and end up injuring themselves; (2) lifting is itself a hard workout, so stacking it before/after a running session compromises recovery for the actual run session (violates stress-recovery-adaptation).
- Real periodized lifting programs are considered necessary mainly for **800m/1500m/5000m Olympic-level track athletes**, and even then only alongside a specialist S&C coach.
- For everyone else, the goal is purely **injury-risk reduction**, not performance — Stazza uses just **5 targeted bodyweight-style exercises covering hip-to-toe**, taking no more than 5 minutes total. Minimal, targeted, time-efficient — explicitly not a gym program.
- If an athlete also lifts for another sport or aesthetic reasons (e.g., a combat-sports background, or wanting a muscular physique), the coach must negotiate priority — dial back running intensity/expectations rather than pretend both can be maximized simultaneously.
- Dynamic warm-ups (walking lunges, leg swings, AIS — active isolated stretching) before hard sessions are recommended broadly as injury prevention, distinct from "strength training" per se.
- Runners with any Achilles issues should get those resolved *before* attempting hill reps of any kind (hills + fast work aggravate the Achilles).

## 9. Mental Approach / Mindset

- **"Stress → recovery → adaptation" as a mental frame, not just physiological** — patience with the process (not chasing every session as hard as possible) is itself a skill to be coached; the phrase "the runner always has to have space to grow into" is as much psychological (not needing to prove everything right now) as physiological.
- **Marathon training as narrative, not just data**: a difficult long run isn't just a physiological stimulus, it's "a reservoir of psychological strength to draw upon" late in race day; a long consistent training block builds not just fitness but an athlete's identity as someone who persists through discomfort. Coaches help athletes find meaning in setbacks (reframed as developmental information, not failure) and maintain a long-term perspective through the inevitable rough patches.
- **Guard against both discouragement and overconfidence**: a coach's job includes reassuring an athlete during a rough patch ("this is exactly what we'd expect at this stage") *and* restraining an athlete tempted to accelerate after early success ("let's consolidate these gains before adding more").
- **Emotional counterbalance during peak fatigue and pre-race anxiety** is called out as a distinct coaching function — steadiness when the athlete is volatile, whether that's despair after a setback or over-excitement after a breakthrough.
- **Race-day psychology**: the temptation to "bank time" early (when legs feel fresh, especially on a downhill start) or to decide "today's the day, let's push" right at halfway are the two most common mental traps that wreck otherwise well-executed marathons — both stem from excitement/impatience overriding the pacing plan.
- **Life-balance as a performance factor, especially flagged for competitive women runners**: being obsessively/exclusively focused on running with no other outlets (social life, hobbies) was explicitly called a *warning sign*, not a good sign, for a top female case-study athlete — a balanced life was treated as a positive predictor of both performance and longevity in the sport.
- **Don't push through a body's warning signs to hit a goal race/time** — repeatedly identified (across multiple athlete case studies) as the single most common self-inflicted setback: racing through a niggle, hiding true fatigue/pain from the coach, or training through early injury signals converts a minor, short-term issue into a much longer layoff. The weekly-feedback/communication system exists specifically to catch this early, since athletes are poor self-reporters under competitive pressure.
- **Discipline and pure compliance with the plan** is repeatedly cited as a bigger differentiator of elite outcomes than raw talent — the most decorated case-study athlete in the corpus is explicitly credited as succeeding primarily through never once skipping or modifying a prescribed session, not through exceptional natural gifts.

## 10. Other Recurring Themes

- **Altitude training**: real benefit needs a genuine commitment — Stazza argues 6 weeks minimum (not the "3 weeks" often cited in the literature) to get a proper training stimulus in alongside the adaptation window. The first 7–10 days at altitude must be easy running ONLY (no workouts) — most of the physiological adaptation (~80% in the first ~3 days, another ~10% in the next ~7) happens in this window, and hammering hard sessions during it is the single most common mistake made by visiting (non-altitude-resident) athletes, leading to a wrecked rest of the trip and the false conclusion that "altitude doesn't work for me." After the initial window, gradually blend longer/slower work and short/fast work together (Canova's "funnel" concept) as adaptation builds. Someone living at altitude runs effort-equivalent pace ~10 sec/mile slower than sea level; a visitor can be up to 30 sec/mile slower initially, improving toward that norm over ~2 months. On return to sea level, wait ~7–10 days before racing a marathon/half (allows readaptation + recovery of race-pace turnover) — Kenyans/permanent altitude residents can race within ~3 days, but that rule doesn't apply to visitors. Deliberately programming some shorter/faster reps at a *lower* elevation within an altitude trip helps counteract the "sluggish turnover" altitude otherwise causes.
- **Heat training**: light aerobic work (even walking) in heat is an effective, evidence-backed way to gain heat adaptation; benefits fade fast (largely gone within about a week) but can be "topped up" with renewed heat exposure. Combining heat + altitude (e.g., an elevated desert location) is powerful but must be sequenced carefully — altitude block first, heat-specific top-up right before a hot/humid race, since heat and altitude adaptations decay at different rates and full humidity specifically can't be simulated by dry heat.
- **The "one marathon per year, use a half marathon as the development lever" model** is the throughline behind almost every case study in the corpus — half-marathon (and 10-mile/10K) pace-progression via the Super Base + Bridge system is treated as the actual engine of marathon improvement, with the marathon-specific block itself mainly "cashing in" fitness that's already been built.
- **Training partners/community matter**: a self-organized local group of similarly-paced training partners is repeatedly credited as a meaningful contributor to individual athletes' improvement, beyond the prescribed sessions themselves.
- **Coaching business/pricing norms** (useful context if hiring a coach): individualized weekly (not templated monthly) programming, daily-adjustable, with open communication (not metered by a pricing tier) and regular review calls, should be achievable for a modest monthly fee — treat coaches charging significantly more than that, or who won't communicate freely, or who copy-paste their own training onto every athlete, as red flags.
